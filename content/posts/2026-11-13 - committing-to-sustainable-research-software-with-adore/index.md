## **The conference and the session**

The[ International Research Software Conference (IRSC)](https://www.researchsoft.org/irsc/), hosted by the Research Software Alliance (ReSA), took place on 7–8 September 2026 in Sheffield, UK, immediately before [RSECon26](https://rsecon26.society-rse.org/). It brought together funders, policymakers, infrastructure providers and RSE communities to discuss new activities and results pertaining to research software and the people who build it.

On 8 September, Lieke de Boer ([ZonMw](https://www.zonmw.nl/en)), Marta Teperek ([NWO](https://www.nwo.nl/en/what-does-the-dutch-research-council-do)) and Carlos Martinez-Ortiz ([Netherlands eScience Center](https://www.esciencecenter.nl/)) ran a Birds of a Feather (BoF) session on **Committing to Sustainable Research Software with ADORE**. The[ Amsterdam Declaration on Funding Research Software Sustainability (ADORE.software)](https://adore.software) sets out recommendations on research software practice, ecosystem, personnel, and ethics, and can be signed by organisations and individuals. The BoF session brought together existing ADORE signatories, prospective adopters, and those interested in cultural change towards sustainable research software. Participants shared their experiences, questions, and lessons learned related to ADORE.

The organisers opened with an[ update from the Dutch signatories,](https://adore.software/2026/07/dutch-adore.software-signatories-share-progress/) who came together to discuss their ADORE-related work earlier in the year. Despite a lot of progress that has been made by the signatories in putting the commitment into practice, they also identified some shared challenges, related to sustainable staffing (in particular in the face of budget cuts in academia), the need for clearer institutional policy frameworks for research software, and recognising software as a first-class research output.

The Dutch update was followed by reflections from other ADORE signatories on their achievements and challenges.


## **Highlights from the lightning talks**

**Daniel S. Katz from the [National Center for Supercomputing Applications (NCSA)](https://www.ncsa.illinois.edu/) at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign** reflected that when he wanted the University to sign ADORE, he quickly realised that a university-wide signature was unlikely given the university's distributed decision-making process. Hence, Dan looked for a level where signing was possible: the NCSA director agreed with the principles and signed for the center. This was a clear external signal, demonstrating NCSA's experience and expertise in and commitment to research software. However, Dan reflected that signing ADORE had relatively minimal internal impact across the university so far.

**Ockmer Oosthuizen from the [Digital Research Alliance of Canada ](https://www.alliancecan.ca/en)** explained that the main challenge in Canada was the fragmented ownership of nationally-essential research software. For example, in a federated country, no single organisation owns the long-term future of a national research platform, and expertise is lost when short-term staff leave. Ockmer reflected that the signing of ADORE provided a key input into the Alliance's Research Software Strategy and directly shaped (recommendations 2 and 5) two new funds: one for Research Software AI Enhancement, and a Research Software Maintenance Fund modelled on the Institute for Research Software's programme.

**Selim Kalayci from [ELIXIR](https://elixir-europe.org/)** explained that no convincing was needed for ELIXIR to decide to sign ADORE; ELIXIR’s existing work already mapped onto ADORE's priorities. In other words: nothing new needed to be done. However, signing the declaration made existing commitments explicit.

**Benedikt Wirth from the [German Research Foundation (DFG)](https://www.dfg.de/en)** explained that for a public funder, signing any declaration involves many people and processes, and for ADORE, it also required resolving a lack of clarity about two of the recommendations. So it took a bit of effort to sign the declaration. However, the signing was worthwhile and acted as a catalyst for many subsequent activities at DFG:



* The e-Research Technologies programme was extended to support further development of widely used research software.
* A new funding programme, Research Software Infrastructures, was set up.
* DFG published guidelines on handling research software in its funding activities, which apply to all DFG-funded projects.
* DFG collaborated on Science Europe's policy framework on developing and aligning policies on research software.

While the challenges described in all four case studies differed, there are some general take-aways:



* If it is difficult to find support at a large organisational level, it might be possible to find the right scale, which fits the context.
* It often may not be necessary to start from scratch in implementing ADORE's recommendations because some existing work might already be aligned with ADORE.
* The benefits of signing ADORE will differ depending on the nature of the organisation: it might make existing commitments explicit, or help define the strategy for the entire organisation.


## **Discussion**

After the short presentation from the ADORE signatories the audience answered a few [Mentimeter questions](./irsc26_adore_software.pdf), which fed into an open discussion.

It turned out that out of 16 respondents, 6 came from organisations already implementing ADORE, but more than half were new to it or still exploring – exactly the audience the session hoped to reach.

When asked about the challenges to signing ADORE, most attendees answered "other", and the discussion unpacked what that meant:



* **Fear and cost of monitoring**: A funder representative explained that ADORE doesn’t really conflict with their policies; the concern is committing to things that then have to be monitored. A response suggested during the discussion is that signing doesn’t have to start with monitoring. ADORE is a set of norms, and signing is first of all a public statement that research software matters, a mark of recognition for the field that shows both those who build and those who use research software that the organisation takes it seriously. 
* **Who has the mandate?** For organisations spanning across several countries (e.g., the European Commission), it is unclear who should sign – the organisation itself, or member states? Similar questions came up for other federated organisations consisting of several individual institutions. The advice was to aim for the highest level at which a signature is possible. If an organisation-wide signature is out of reach, a signature at a lower level, such as a centre or institute, still sends a clear signal, as the NCSA example showed. And if signing as an organisation proves really difficult, you can sign as an individual. 

The participants were in agreement that clearer communication about the benefits to raise awareness and inform colleagues could go a long way at addressing most of the challenges discussed.


### **Making signing easy**

Subsequently, the participants discussed what would make signing ADORE a no-brainer. Their ideas included:



* **Link it to funding**: make an ADORE commitment part of funding requirements. The idea of requiring signatures in funding applications sparked debate. ADORE is more a set of norms than a policy requirement, and without baseline awareness and conviction, a requirement may come too early. At the same time, the toolkit includes recommendations on funding requirements, and there is precedent: funders requiring data sharing nudged institutions to develop research data policies, and the same could work for software.
* **Build leadership momentum**: create enthusiasm at the senior leadership level, lobby overarching organisations (such as Science Europe for European funders) to champion it, and make clear to decision makers that the opportunities outweigh the risks.
* **Show, don't tell**: build and provide case studies from similar organisations (peer pressure and cross-organisational competition), an elevator pitch on the benefits, and an alignment toolkit showing how ADORE already matches existing policies.

Finally, signatories were again asked to describe the key benefits of signing ADORE. They reflected that signing ADORE:



* guides direction and feeds into organisational strategy;
* aligns different interpretations of what "sustainable research software" means;
* reinforces the organisation's course and makes it less volatile;
* helps fund software activities; and
* serves as a reminder and a tool to convince the organisation that this matters.


## **Key takeaways and next steps**



* **Start where you can.** A centre, institute, or individual signature is a legitimate first step when organisation-wide signing isn't feasible.
* **Signing can be a catalyst or a confirmation.** For DFG and the Alliance, it triggered new strategy and funding; for ELIXIR, it recognised work already under way.
* **The main barriers are organisational, not ideological.** Unclear mandates, fear of monitoring and a fragmented landscape came up more often than disagreement with ADORE's content.
* **Better stories are needed.** Case studies, clear benefit statements, and an alignment toolkit would make it easier for newcomers to make the case internally.
